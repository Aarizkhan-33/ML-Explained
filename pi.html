Visualizing Data: Polygons & Pie Charts

Welcome! Today we are learning how to take raw data—like the number of patients in a clinic—and turn it into visual shapes. We will focus on two methods: the Frequency Polygon and the Pie Chart.

🧠 The "Why": Why do we visualize data? Cognitive Power
Because the human brain processes shapes and angles much faster than it processes lists of raw numbers. Visuals help us instantly answer "how" data is distributed and "why" certain categories dominate.

📐 Frequency Polygon

Connects class midpoints into a closed shape to visualize continuous trend distributions.

🥧 Pie Chart

Slices a complete 360° circle into proportional wedges to show parts of a 100% whole.

The Frequency Polygon

📖 Define: What is a Polygon?
The word "Polygon" breaks down into "poly" meaning many, and "gon" meaning angles or sides. A polygon is always a closed figure.
🔨 Explain (The "How"): Building the Polygon
How do we build it? Imagine a bar chart (histogram). If we put a single dot at the very top middle of each bar (midpoints) and connect those dots with a straight line, we get a frequency polygon.
⚓ The "Why": Why must it be closed?
To show that the data has a clear beginning and end. We ground the line by bringing the very first and very last points down to the baseline (the zero mark on the x-axis).
This proves mathematically that frequencies outside this range are zero, sealing the total area beneath the curve.

The Pie Chart — Concept & Rules

🥧 Define: The Pie Chart
A Pie Chart is a round chart or circle that represents exactly 360 degrees ($360^\circ$).
🔪 Explain (The "How"): Slicing the Whole
How does it work? We take our total data and slice the $360^\circ$ circle into pieces. Each category receives a slice whose angle directly reflects its share of the total.
⭐ The Golden Rules
  1. Slices are not allowed to overlap.
  2. There can be no empty space left in the circle.
⚖️ The "Why": Why use 360°?
Because a circle is mathematically $360^\circ$. If our total data is 100%, then 100% of our data must perfectly equal $360^\circ$ of visual space.
If slices overlapped or had gaps, our data would be mathematically incorrect—implying double-counting or lost patients!

The Pie Chart — Breaking Down the Math

Reveal: Formula Step 1: Total Step 2: LBP Step 3: Sciatica Step 4: Stroke Step 5: CP
🏥 The Scenario & The Master Formula Clinical Patient Data
We have patient data categorized into: Lower Back Pain (LBP), Sciatica (S), Stroke (ST), and Cerebral Palsy (CP).
$$Angle = \left(\frac{\text{Obtained}}{\text{Total}}\right) \times 360$$

Why this formula? We divide the obtained number by the total to find the fraction. We then multiply by 360 to find out how much of the circle's $360^\circ$ belongs to that fraction.

Step 1: Find the Total Denominator
Before we can slice anything, we need the whole pie:
LBP (250) + Sciatica (60) + Stroke (150) + CP (30) = Total 490 patients.

Our total is 490. Every single category will now be divided by 490.

Step 2: Calculate LBP Angle Slice 1
Formula: $\left(\frac{250}{490}\right) \times 360 = \mathbf{184^\circ}$

💡 Teacher's Insight: Because 250 is roughly half of 490, it takes up roughly half the circle ($180^\circ$).

Step 3: Calculate Sciatica Angle Slice 2
Formula: $\left(\frac{60}{490}\right) \times 360 = \mathbf{44^\circ}$

A smaller wedge ($44^\circ$) reflecting 60 out of 490 patients.

Step 4: Calculate Stroke Angle Slice 3
Formula: $\left(\frac{150}{490}\right) \times 360 = \mathbf{110^\circ}$

Stroke represents 150 patients, commanding a substantial $110^\circ$ slice.

Step 5: Calculate CP Angle Slice 4 (Seals Circle)
Formula: $\left(\frac{30}{490}\right) \times 360 = \mathbf{22^\circ}$

🎯 Check the Sum: $184^\circ + 44^\circ + 110^\circ + 22^\circ = \mathbf{360^\circ}$! The circle is perfectly sealed!

Conceptual Exam Questions & Short Answers

Question 1 Click card to reveal
Why is it a strict mathematical requirement that a Frequency Polygon must be a "closed figure"?
Answer: A frequency polygon must be closed (anchored to the x-axis at both ends) to accurately represent the total area under the curve. By closing the figure, it proves that there are zero frequencies beyond the measured range, giving the data a definitive start and end point.
Question 2 Click card to reveal
In a Pie Chart, how does the formula $(\text{Obtained} / \text{Total}) \times 360$ act as a translation tool?
Answer: The formula translates raw data into visual geometry. By dividing the Obtained value by the Total, you find the exact ratio or percentage of the whole. Multiplying that ratio by $360$ translates that percentage into literal degrees, ensuring the data perfectly maps onto a physical circle.
Question 3 Click card to reveal
Why are overlaps and empty spaces strictly forbidden when constructing a Pie Chart?
Answer: A Pie Chart represents exactly 100% of a dataset. If slices overlapped, it would imply that a single data point belongs to two distinct categories at once, ruining the proportions. If there was empty space, it would mean the data does not add up to the total, violating the rule that the whole circle ($360^\circ$) equals the entire dataset.
